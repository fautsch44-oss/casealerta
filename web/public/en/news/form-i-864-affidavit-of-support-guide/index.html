Form I-864 Affidavit of Support for a green card: a simple guide

What Form I-864 is, who can sponsor, the 125% income rule, joint sponsors, Form I-864A, and when the sponsor's obligation ends.

If a family member is petitioning for your green card, you almost always need Form I-864, Affidavit of Support.

With this form, a person promises the government that they will support you financially so you do not rely on certain public benefits.

Who needs it

  • Most family-based immigrants (for example, through Form I-130).
  • Some employment-based cases, when a relative files the petition or owns a significant part of the petitioning company.
  • There are exceptions. For example, if the immigrant already has 40 quarters of work in the U.S. That exemption is claimed with Form I-864W or as the instructions explain.

Who can be a sponsor

  • A U.S. citizen, U.S. national, or lawful permanent resident.
  • At least 18 years old.
  • Domiciled (living) in the U.S. or a U.S. territory.
  • In family cases, the main sponsor is the person who filed the petition.

The income requirement

  • The sponsor must show income of at least 125% of the Federal Poverty Guidelines for their household size.
  • Active-duty military members sponsoring a spouse or child only need 100%.
  • The guidelines change every year. Always use the current chart, Form I-864P.
  • If income falls short, assets (savings, property) can count under the rules in the instructions.

Joint sponsors and Form I-864A

  • If the main sponsor does not meet the income level, another person can be a joint sponsor. They file their own I-864 and must meet the same requirements and the income level on their own.
  • A relative in the same household can combine income using Form I-864A. That relative also becomes legally bound.
  • Form I-864EZ is a short version for simple cases; the instructions explain when it can be used.

What documents to include

  • Proof of income, such as the most recent federal tax return (or an IRS transcript) and W-2s.
  • Proof of the sponsor's status (citizenship or permanent residence).
  • The form instructions have the full list. Check it before you send.

When the obligation ends

The sponsor's obligation continues even after a divorce. It ends when the immigrant:

  • Becomes a U.S. citizen.
  • Earns 40 quarters of work (about 10 years).
  • Gives up permanent residence and leaves the U.S. permanently.
  • Dies. It also ends if the sponsor dies (though debts already owed may remain).

Where it is filed

  • If you adjust status inside the U.S., it goes with your Form I-485.
  • If you process through a consulate, it goes to the State Department's National Visa Center (NVC).

What to do

  • Always download the current edition of the form and instructions at uscis.gov/i-864.
  • Check this year's I-864P income chart before filling it out.
  • If you are below 125%, find a joint sponsor or add a household member's income with Form I-864A.
  • Sign by hand where required and keep a copy of everything.
  • Track your case with your receipt number in CaseAlerta to know when its status changes.
